Concept: Culture

Meaning: the shared ideas, beliefs, art, tools and ways of living of a group of people. Features: language, religion, food, technology, stories. Examples: the way a city builds its libraries; the tools people invent. Non-examples: one person’s mood; the weather.

Concept: Identity

Meaning: how a person or group sees who they are and what they belong to. Features: “we” and “they” stories, pride, what a group claims as its own. Examples: a country saying “we invented this.” Non-examples: a fact no one feels anything about.

Our unit runs on one key concept. Decide which examples are global interactions (ideas, goods or people crossing between cultures) and which are not. Click a card to sort it.

Global interactions = the way ideas, goods, people and beliefs move between cultures, changing each other along the way. The “yes” cards all involve something crossing from one culture to another. That crossing is what this whole unit is about.

3The provocation: who named the “Dark Ages”?

The phrase “Dark Ages” was coined by a later European writer (the poet Petrarch, in the 1300s) who thought the centuries after Rome were a cultural “darkness” compared with ancient Greece and Rome. Later writers stretched the label across roughly 500–1000 CE.

Part of what looked “dark” was simply that fewer written records in Latin survived from Western Europe — dark from one viewpoint. At the very same time, cities like Baghdad and Córdoba were bright with libraries, science and trade. So the label tells us as much about who was doing the naming as about the age itself.

Your commission

A museum is opening a new gallery: The Remix Room. Its big idea — many inventions we’re told were “born” in the European Renaissance and Enlightenment were really built over centuries, by many cultures, and remixed as they travelled.

You are a guest curator. Over this unit you’ll research one idea and design its exhibit for museum visitors. Everything we study from here is raw material for it.

1See it modelled: one idea’s real journey

Take an idea we usually credit to Europe — the scientific experiment.

“Modern science began in Europe in the 1600s with thinkers like Galileo, who tested ideas by experiment instead of trusting old books.”

Around 1000 CE in Cairo, Ibn al-Haytham wrote the Book of Optics, insisting ideas be tested by experiment. His work was translated into Latin and studied in Europe for centuries — well before Galileo. The experiment didn’t appear from nowhere in Europe; it travelled and grew across cultures. That is exactly the kind of story your exhibit will tell.

Fair warning. The point is not “one culture invented everything.” It’s that big ideas are almost always built by many cultures exchanging over time. Keep that balance in your exhibit.

    Where a lot of these journeys begin

    Meet the age some people once called “dark” — and others call a Golden Age.

    1The House of Wisdom

    In Baghdad, from the 700s–800s CE, scholars gathered at a centre of learning often called the House of Wisdom. They ran a huge translation movement, turning Greek, Persian and Indian works into Arabic — then building on them in maths, medicine, astronomy and optics.

    Knowledge here was transmitted and advanced across cultures — not invented from nothing. Centuries later, many of these Arabic works were translated into Latin (in places like Toledo and Sicily) and helped spark the European Renaissance. That’s global interactions running across 700 years.
